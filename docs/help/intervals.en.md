# Why the same note is not always the same pitch

## The thing this exercise trains

A written F♯ is a **major third** over a D bass. For that third to ring rather
than beat, it has to sit low. The **same** written F♯ is a **fifth** over a B
bass — and sounded that low, the fifth beats instead.

Nothing on the page has changed. The note moves, and moving it is most of what
playing in tune means on a baroque flute. A tuner cannot teach this: it would
call both soundings "F♯" and both "wrong", because it knows one pitch per note
and this music does not work that way.

## What the numbers are

Computed by this app, from the temperament and reference pitch you have set —
not quoted from anywhere. In Vallotti at A = 415, with the note as the mediant
of each key:

| key | note | as a third over | as a fifth over | must rise |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| D | F♯ | D | B | 9.8 cents |
| G | B | G | E | 9.8 cents |
| A | C♯ | A | F♯ | 13.7 cents |
| C | E | C | A | 9.8 cents |
| F | A | F | D | 9.8 cents |

In **equal temperament** every one of these is 15.6 cents, because equal
temperament places all the basses identically. Under a historical temperament
the amount varies with where that temperament puts the two bass notes, which
is worth noticing: the correction is not a fixed quantity you learn once.

The exercise measures the **difference between your two soundings**, never
your distance from a tuner. That is deliberate. A reference pitch set wrong, a
flute sitting sharp, a player warming up over ten minutes — all of them move
both notes together and cancel out. What is left is the only thing being
asked: did the note move, which way, and how far.

## Sharps are low and flats are high

This is the part that most surprises a modern player, and it is the reverse of
the modern instinct.

In this repertoire a **flat sits above the enharmonic sharp**: B♭ is *higher*
than A♯, E♭ higher than D♯. It follows from the harmony rather than from
taste — a flat is usually approached as a descending step or sits as a minor
third, while a sharp is usually a leading note pulling up into the note above
and belongs to a pure major third below it.

The convention **inverted during the nineteenth century**, when melodic
thinking displaced harmonic: from then on a sharp is played high and a flat
low, which is what most players are taught now.

Two pieces of evidence that this was practised rather than theorised:

- **Quantz added a second key to the flute** in the middle of the eighteenth
  century for exactly this — to separate E♭ from D♯. The distinction was worth
  building hardware for.
- **Charles Burney**, travelling and writing in the 1770s, describes singers
  making audible distinctions between sharps and flats, and is impressed by
  the ones who do it well.

This app takes the distinction seriously throughout: pitches are spelled
(letter, alteration, octave) and never reduced to a piano-key number, so D♯
and E♭ can be, and are, different notes.

## How it is taught, according to the people who teach it

Consistently, and not by looking at anything: by playing with other people, by
playing the bass and the inner parts rather than only the top line, and by
learning to be drawn to the colour of a chord that rings. The drone in this
exercise is a stand-in for the bass player you do not have in the room.

Tromlitz, at the end of the eighteenth century, goes further and tells orchestral
violinists **not** to follow the harpsichord slavishly, since they too can make
these fine distinctions and the keyboard cannot.
