# Why octave width can't be zeroed everywhere at once

Moving the stopper (cork) is the standard way to true up octaves on any transverse
flute, baroque or modern. But its effect is not even across the range — and that
uneven effect, not a flaw in any particular instrument, is why one stopper position
always leaves some octaves wider or narrower than others.

## The stopper's effect grows sharply as you go up the range

The clearest demonstration of this comes from a controlled trial by flute maker Terry
McGee, who moved the stopper in 5mm steps and compared the same notes across three
octaves. The result: **the lowest octave barely moved at all, the second octave shifted
moderately (more so toward its top), and the third octave moved the most** — with
stopper movement toward the embouchure hole consistently sharpening the higher notes
each time ([McGee, "Effect of Stopper Position"](https://www.mcgee-flutes.com/Stopper.html)).

In other words: the cork is a strong lever on the top of the range and a weak one on
the bottom. A single position cannot fully correct both ends at once — some residual
width or narrowness at one extreme is the unavoidable cost of correcting the other.

## This is not a new problem — it's the same one Quantz and Tromlitz worked with

Adjustable cork position for octave tuning goes back to the origin of the modern
one-keyed traverso. Johann Joachim Quantz is credited with fitting the head joint with
a screw-adjustable cork around 1752, specifically so octaves could be trued without
otherwise disturbing the instrument's intonation; his own stated criterion for correct
cork placement was simply that the octaves, taken together, come out true. Johann
George Tromlitz's 1791 flute tutor likewise treats the screw-cork and the tuning of the
second octave as a standing topic — evidence that players were already working with
exactly this trade-off, by ear, well over two centuries ago.

## Bore taper widens octaves too, independent of the cork

Separately from stopper position, a more strongly conical bore produces wider octaves
as a structural matter. This is documented directly by woodwind maker and researcher
Jan Bouterse from measurement and build experience with historical winds
([Bouterse, *Making Woodwind Instruments*, FoMRHI Comm. 2097](https://www.fomrhi.org/vanilla/fomrhi/uploads/bulletins/Fomrhi-143/Comm%202097.pdf)).
A traverso's bore is meaningfully more conical than a modern cylindrical flute's, so
some baseline tendency toward a wide low octave is arguably built into the instrument
type itself — before the stopper is even considered.

## A wide low octave is not automatically a flaw

In flute acoustics research on modern instruments, a stretch of roughly 20–35 cents
between the fundamental and the next resonance in the lowest octave is treated as
generally desirable rather than a defect — it aids the tone's harmonic response, and it
offsets the tendency of the fundamental register (but not the octave above it) to
sharpen under louder blowing, so a slightly wide resting octave converges toward true
under normal playing dynamics (Coltman; discussed in Benade, *Fundamentals of Musical
Acoustics*). This figure comes from cylindrical Boehm-flute research and may not map
exactly onto a conical traverso, but the underlying mechanism — breath-speed asymmetry
between registers — is general.

## The practical target is a weighted compromise, not a flat average

Because no single stopper position can zero every register, standard guidance —
historical and modern alike — is to prioritize the part of the range actually played
most, rather than average uniformly across the whole compass. One classic formulation
is to set the stopper so the octave overblows most accurately around the middle of the
working range, treating the extremes as the accepted trade-off (Trevor Robinson, *The
Amateur Wind Instrument Maker*, discussed in
[St. John, "Acoustics for Flutemaking," FoMRHI Comm. 2131](https://www.fomrhi.org/vanilla/fomrhi/uploads/bulletins/Fomrhi-150/Comm%202131.pdf)).

## The highest register has always been the player's job, not the maker's

Standard acoustics literature notes that players routinely use embouchure and breath to
correct pitch by ear, and that this is used most on the highest notes specifically,
because they are not expected to be the purest in intonation straight from the
instrument (C.J. Nederveen, *Acoustical Aspects of Woodwind Instruments*). Pitch-purity
expectations were never uniform across the range — the top register has been treated as
the player's responsibility since Quantz and Tromlitz's own time.
