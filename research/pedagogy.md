# What the evidence says about teaching intonation and ear

Researched 7 September 2026 for the design of this app. Verification status
is carried through: each citation was confirmed by reading the source or its
publisher abstract unless marked otherwise. **"(not verified)" means exactly
that, and nothing so marked should be quoted in the app until it is read.**
The "do not claim" list at the end is the most useful part for keeping the
app honest.

The question put to the research: the app already uses drone-based exercises,
shows no needle during a note and a reading only afterwards, compares a
player against their own best rather than a standard, and refuses
gamification. Is any of that supported, and what would the evidence add?

## 1. Augmented feedback and the guidance hypothesis

**What the literature says**

- **Feedback that helps performance during practice can hurt learning.** Salmoni, Schmidt & Walter (1984), *Psychological Bulletin* 95, 355–386, reframed knowledge of results (KR) as *guidance*: it props up performance while present and, if given too often or too immediately, leaves the learner dependent on it. The measure that matters is a delayed retention test *without* feedback, not performance during practice. Soderstrom & Bjork (2015), *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 10, 176–199, make the same point for learning generally: performance during practice is an unreliable index of learning, and some manipulations move the two in opposite directions.
- **Less frequent, delayed, and summarised KR produces better retention than every-trial, instantaneous KR** (all lab tasks):
  - Winstein & Schmidt (1990), *JEP: Learning, Memory & Cognition* 16, 677–691: 50% KR gave better retention than 100% KR, although acquisition performance was worse.
  - Swinnen, Schmidt, Nicholson & Shapiro (1990), *JEP:LMC* 16, 706–716: instantaneous KR degraded learning relative to KR delayed by a few seconds; their explanation is that instant KR blocks the learner's own evaluation of intrinsic feedback, so error-detection ability never develops.
  - Schmidt, Young, Swinnen & Shapiro (1989), *JEP:LMC* 15, 352–359: summary KR (results withheld until a block of trials is done) improved retention; the best summary length for their task was ~15 trials.
  - Sherwood (1988), *Perceptual and Motor Skills* 66, 535–542: bandwidth KR (feedback only when error exceeds a tolerance) produced more consistent movement on transfer; the wider 10% band beat 5% and every-trial KR. Lee & Carnahan (1990), *QJEP* 42A, showed the bandwidth benefit is not merely a frequency effect (citation verified; content from secondary summary).
  - Schmidt & Wulf (1997), *Human Factors* 39, 509–525: *continuous concurrent* visual feedback during the movement improved performance in practice but interfered with learning an accurate, stable movement pattern on next-day retention.
  - Textbook synthesis: Schmidt, Lee, Winstein, Wulf & Zelaznik, *Motor Control and Learning: A Behavioral Emphasis*, 6th ed., Human Kinetics, 2019.
- **The caveat: this evidence is from simple lab tasks.** Wulf & Shea (2002), *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 9, 185–211, argue that principles from simple skills do not generalise cleanly to complex ones, and that "more frequent feedback might be required for the learning of complex skills," at least early on. Sigrist, Rauter, Riener & Wolf (2013), *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 20, 21–53, review augmented visual/auditory/haptic feedback and treat concurrent feedback as potentially useful for complex tasks early in learning, with the guidance-hypothesis risk of dependency as the reason to fade it (citation verified; I could not retrieve the body this session, so treat the "complex tasks early" reading as my recollection of the review, not a quotation).
- **Feedback after good trials, and learner-chosen feedback: promising but shaky.** Chiviacowsky & Wulf (2002, 2005) found learners prefer feedback after trials they judged good; Chiviacowsky & Wulf (2007), *Research Quarterly for Exercise and Sport* 78, 40–47, found KR after good trials produced better learning than KR after poor trials. However, St. Germain et al. (2022), *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* (N = 228), found no self-controlled-feedback advantage and no effect on autonomy, competence or intrinsic motivation; Bacelar et al. (2022), *Psychology of Sport and Exercise* 59, likewise found no self-controlled benefit; and McKay, Bacelar, Parma, Miller & Carter (2023), *International Review of Sport and Exercise Psychology*, conclude that reporting bias and underpowered designs have "substantially exaggerated" the benefits of self-controlled practice and enhanced expectancies. A 2024 review in *Psychology of Sport and Exercise* found that of 166 OPTIMAL-theory experiments only 21% measured motivation at all, and only 8 found group-level motivation effects.

**Is the app's "no needle during, reading after" supported?** Yes, on the general principle: a live needle is continuous concurrent visual feedback, exactly the condition Schmidt & Wulf (1997) and the Swinnen delay result argue against, and the reason is directly the app's goal (the player must develop their *own* error detection, i.e. their ear). Two honest qualifications: (a) the music-specific tests are thin and mixed (see §2); (b) a reading after *every* note is still 100%-frequency terminal KR, which the same literature says is not optimal for retention.

## 2. Visual feedback for intonation specifically

- **Pardue & McPherson (2019), *Frontiers in Psychology* 10:627.** 12 beginning violinists, four in-situ lessons each with aural feedback, visual feedback, both, or none. No significant accuracy difference between conditions (p = 0.48); large individual variation, "cases where the feedback was distracting and intonation suffered"; "half the students expressed that visuals were distracting due to feedback changing too rapidly"; participants looked at the display only 16% of the time. Authors explicitly did not test retention after removal.
- **Blanco, Tassani & Ramirez (2021), *Frontiers in Psychology* 12:684693.** RCT, 47 beginners plus 15 experts, violin and voice pitch-matching with baseline / acquisition / transfer (no-feedback) phases. The real-time *visual* feedback group improved and *retained* the gain at transfer; the auditory-guide group improved only while the guide was present. This is the one music study that directly tests removal, and it cuts against a naive guidance-hypothesis prediction for visual feedback, in a short pitch-matching task with beginners.
- **Schlegel & Springer (2018), *International Journal of Music Education*.** High-school (n = 29) and college (n = 30) trombonists tuned to a recorded G3 with a tuner giving accurate or deliberately inaccurate readings, then rated confidence. I could retrieve the design but not the results (not verified); do not cite outcomes from it without reading it. The design itself is worth noting: it asks whether players trust a display over their ears.
- **Yarbrough, Karrick & Morrison (1995), *JRME* 43, 232–241.** 197 wind players; telling them the direction of mistuning did not improve tuning accuracy; only years of instruction did.
- **Morrison (2000), *JRME* 48.** Two experiments; students with 1–7+ years of experience matched target pitches inside melodies; errors were most often sharp (citation verified; ERIC abstract only).
- **Springer (2020), *Update: Applications of Research in Music Education*, "Research to Resource: Evidence-Based Strategies for Improving Wind Intonation"** summarises this JRME line (ensemble tuning, vocalisation, tuner use, timbral instruction). Abstract verified; body not retrieved.
- **Kopiez (2003), *Music Perception* 20, 383–410.** Two professional trumpeters playing the top voice against accompaniments in adaptive just intonation vs equal temperament produced essentially the same intonation in both (mean deviations 4.9 vs 6.7 cents): an unconscious "always the same" strategy, even in experts. Springer et al. (2021, below) likewise found most collegiate performances closer to equal temperament than to just intonation.

**Findings to act on:** real-time displays are distracting for a meaningful fraction of players and demand visual attention that competes with listening; the only removal test in music found visual feedback gains survived removal, so "displays always create dependence" is not established; and telling a player which way they are out, before they play, does not help; experience does.

## 3. Ear training, audiation, singing, drones

- **Gordon's Music Learning Theory.** Gordon coined "audiation" (1975) for hearing and comprehending music that is not physically present; the theory is set out in *Learning Sequences in Music: Skill, Content, and Patterns* (GIA, latest ed. 2012). The empirical base (reviewed by Grashel 1991, *Update* 10(1)) is mostly aptitude measurement and tonal/rhythm achievement in children; Platz, Kopiez, Lehmann & Wolf (2022), *Music & Science*, question whether the Advanced Measures of Music Audiation measure audiation or tonal memory. I found no controlled study showing MLT-based instruction improves *instrumental intonation* in adults.
- **Singing/vocalisation for instrumentalists.** Elliott (1974), *JRME* 22, 120–128: daily vocalisation in beginning band improved a *sense-of-pitch* (perception) test. Bernhard (2003), *Update* 22(1), reviewing the field: singing studies "devoid of context" showed no effect on performed intonation; Schlacks (1981, Univ. of Miami dissertation; interval-training vocalisation, 20 days) found no pre/post pitch-accuracy difference (via Bernhard; Bennett 1994 cited there, not verified). Silvey, Nápoles & Springer (2019), *JRME* 66: singing before tuning (N = 72) was numerically best but not significant. Steenstrup et al. (2021), *Frontiers in Psychology* 12:757052: 50 trumpet students; physical practice and combined mental+singing+physical practice improved pitch accuracy vs no practice, and only the combined condition improved expression. Palese & Duke (2022), *Update* 41, 34–44: experienced players spend at least as long imagining an ideal performance as playing it; novices barely pause.
- **Drones.** Springer, Silvey, Nápoles & Warnet (2021), *JRME* 68: 68 collegiate wind players, melody with tonic drone / tonic+fifth drone / no drone: no significant intonation difference, yet 60% believed the dyad drone gave their best intonation. Zabanal (2019), *String Research Journal* 9: 28 school violin/viola players, pretest / drone posttest / unaccompanied posttest: no significant difference. Zabanal (2020), *Update*: collegiate string players widely use and believe in drones. Morgan (2022, Univ. of Mississippi dissertation, N = 58 school string players): no significant group × time interaction; a secondary t-test favoured drone-only over drone+singing. Note what these tested: an immediate effect within one session, not weeks of drone practice. There is, as far as I could find, no longitudinal trial of drone practice.

**Findings to act on:** singing/audiating is plausible and cheap, with the best evidence for perception and for *combined* imagery+singing+playing, not for singing alone; the drone's benefit is asserted by pedagogy and believed by players but not demonstrated in short experiments; players' self-assessment of intonation is unreliable in the direction of over-confidence with a drone.

## 4. Motivation without gamification

- **Self-determination theory.** Ryan & Deci (2000), *American Psychologist* 55, 68–78 (well-known; not re-verified this session); Evans (2015), *Musicae Scientiae* 19, 65–83, applies autonomy/competence/relatedness to music learning and argues music-motivation research needs this unified frame. Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999), *Psychological Bulletin* 125, 627–668 (128 studies): expected tangible rewards undermined free-choice intrinsic motivation (engagement-, completion-, performance-contingent d = −0.40, −0.36, −0.28); informational verbal feedback that supports competence does not.
- **Feedback framing.** Kluger & DeNisi (1996), *Psychological Bulletin* 119, 254–284: across 607 effect sizes feedback helped on average (d = 0.41) but over a third of interventions *reduced* performance; feedback that directs attention to the self rather than the task is the culprit. Hattie & Timperley (2007), *Review of Educational Research* 77, 81–112: feedback at task, process and self-regulation levels is effective; praise aimed at the person is the least effective level. Henderlong & Lepper (2002), *Psychological Bulletin* 128, 774–795: praise helps when it attributes outcomes to controllable causes, supports autonomy, "enhances competence without an overreliance on social comparisons," and conveys attainable standards.
- **Dweck, with caveats.** Mueller & Dweck (1998), *JPSP* 75, 33–52: intelligence praise vs effort praise in fifth-graders. But: Sisk et al. (2018), *Psychological Science* 29, 549–571 (273 studies / 43 interventions): weak effects overall; Yeager et al. (2019), *Nature* 573: a one-hour intervention raised lower-achieving ninth-graders' GPA by 0.10 points; Burnette et al. (2023), *Psychological Bulletin*: targeted, high-fidelity interventions d = 0.14 for achievement with a prediction interval spanning zero; Macnamara & Burgoyne (2023), *Psychological Bulletin*: 94% of studies confounded, financial-incentive authors 2.5× more likely to report positive effects, higher-quality studies less likely to show benefit. Process-focused wording is harmless and consistent with Henderlong & Lepper; do not claim it changes outcomes.
- **Flow.** Csikszentmihalyi (1990), *Flow*, Harper & Row; challenge–skill balance is one of nine dimensions. O'Neill (1999), *Bulletin of the CRME* 141, 129–134: 60 young musicians, experience sampling; high achievers reported flow more often (correlational). Tan & Sin (2021), *Musicae Scientiae*, systematic review of flow in music.
- **Deliberate practice.** Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer (1993), *Psychological Review* 100, 363–406: retrospective practice estimates tracked violinists' level; they also reviewed evidence of "essentially no benefit from durations exceeding 4 hr per day," reduced benefit beyond 2 hr, and effective deliberate practice closer to 1 hr sessions with recovery. Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald (2014), *Psychological Science* 25, 1608–1618: deliberate practice explains 21% of performance variance in music (26% games, 18% sports, 4% education, <1% professions); Hambrick et al. (2014), *Intelligence* 45: about a third of *reliable* variance in music and chess. Ericsson disputed the coding of "deliberate" practice in those meta-analyses (rebuttals in *Intelligence* and later; not re-verified).
- **Gamification, the critical evidence.** Hanus & Fox (2015), *Computers & Education* 80, 152–161: a 16-week course with badges and a leaderboard showed *less* motivation, satisfaction and empowerment over time and lower exam scores, mediated by lost intrinsic motivation. Mekler, Brühlmann, Tuch & Opwis (2017), *Computers in Human Behavior* 71, 525–534: points/levels/leaderboards raised output but did not change competence or intrinsic motivation; they "acted as extrinsic incentives." Toda, Valle & Isotani (2018), Springer CCIS 832, "The Dark Side of Gamification": catalogue of documented negative effects (demotivation, rule confusion, dislike of penalties, worse transfer). Hamari, Koivisto & Sarsa (2014), HICSS: effects "greatly dependent on the context... as well as on the users." Sailer & Homner (2020), *Educational Psychology Review* 32, 77–112, meta-analysis: small positive averages (cognitive g = 0.49, motivational g = 0.36, behavioural g = 0.25), but motivational and behavioural effects were *not stable* in the high-rigour subset. The defensible claim is therefore not "gamification harms" but "gamification is unnecessary here, its motivational benefit is unstable under rigorous testing, and the SDT/reward literature gives a mechanism (contingent rewards, social comparison) by which it can undermine exactly the intrinsic motivation this player already has."

## 5. Practice structure

- **Interleaving / contextual interference in music.** Stambaugh (2011), *JRME* 58, 368–383: 41 beginning clarinettists; blocked and random groups equal at end of practice, but at retention the random group was significantly faster (F(1,38) = 24.95, p < .001) and the blocked group had *slowed* since practice. Carter & Grahn (2016), *Frontiers in Psychology* 7:1251: 10 advanced clarinettists; interleaving marginal-to-significant advantage depending on rater, yet most participants *preferred* blocked practice for its feeling of fluency. Abushanab & Bishara (2013), *Memory & Cognition* 41: trained pianists were faster after fixed-order practice immediately but faster after random-order practice two days later, and wrongly predicted the opposite ("illusion of competence"). Mathias & Goldman (2024/25), *JRME* 72, 357–375: 20 advanced violinists, blocked vs random vs increasing-CI schedules (results not retrieved; not verified). General: Kornell & Bjork (2008), *Psychological Science* 19, 585–592 (interleaving aids induction; participants judged massing better even after it lost); Bjork (1994) "desirable difficulties," in Metcalfe & Shimamura (eds.) *Metacognition*, MIT Press.
- **Spacing and sleep.** Rubin-Rabson (1940), *J. Educational Psychology* 31, 270–284: practice spread over two days beat massed practice and same-day distribution for piano memorisation. Cepeda et al. (2006), *Psychological Bulletin* 132, 354–380: optimal gap grows with the retention interval (verbal tasks). Simmons & Duke (2006), *JRME* 54, 257–269: sleep between sessions enhances keyboard-melody motor skill. Caution: Wiseheart, D'Souza & Chae (2017), *PLoS ONE* 12(8): no within-session spacing effect (0–15 min lags) for a piano sequence, so spacing claims should be about days, not minutes.
- **Quality over quantity.** Duke, Simmons & Cash (2009), *JRME* 56, 310–321: among 17 advanced pianists, retention ranking was unrelated to practice time or number of trials; the best players identified errors precisely, fixed them before moving on, and varied tempo systematically.
- **Self-assessment and reflection.** Hewitt (2001), *JRME* 49, 307–322: junior-high instrumentalists who listened to a model and then self-evaluated improved more than those who did not self-evaluate. Palese & Duke (2022): "thinking time" before and between attempts is a marker of expertise. Swinnen et al. (1990) supply the mechanism: the learner must evaluate the attempt *before* being told.
- **Session length.** Ericsson et al. (1993) as above; Quantz (1752) prescribed two hours morning and two afternoon "but also resting a little during the practice" and warned that excess playing "weakens the body and wears out the senses" (see §6).

## 6. Baroque sources and modern traverso pedagogy

- **Quantz, *Versuch einer Anweisung die Flöte traversiere zu spielen* (Berlin, 1752); Reilly's translation *On Playing the Flute* (Faber 1966; Northeastern UP 2001).** Read this session in the Wikisource transcription of the 1752 text; paragraph numbers as transcribed there.
  - Chapter X, "What a beginner must observe in his particular practice": practise two hours in the morning and two in the afternoon, resting during practice ("zwo Stunden Vormittags, und eben so viele Nachmittags... aber auch unter währender Uebung, immer ein wenig ausruhete"); superfluous playing "entkräftet den Leib; man nutzet die Sinne ab"; never play a piece faster than you can keep in one tempo (§3); keep the ear constantly on those playing with you, "besonders auf die Grundstimme" (the bass) (§15); note the key immediately and imitate what you hear "until he is able to play back at once everything he hears" (§20).
  - Chapter IV, on embouchure: correct "the defective notes" "by means of the embouchure and by ear" ("durch Hülfe des Ansatzes, und nach dem Gehöre... rein zu spielen," §23); turn the flute outward/inward and cover the hole more/less (§22–26); a list of notes that are naturally too high (e.g. G#/Ab, low F) or too low (F#) with the specific correction; louder blowing raises pitch, softer lowers it (§22); a good musical ear is prerequisite (§16); make the major thirds "well known to the ear" (§24).
  - Enharmonics and the second key: Quantz's own explanation, quoted in Haynes (1991): "What led me to add another key... was the difference between major and minor semitones"; "the notes indicated with a flat are a comma higher than those indicated with a sharp"; and, on the monochord, that "every singer and instrumentalist should become familiar with its use" so as to "recognize minor semitones much earlier." This is a historical endorsement of a *measuring device used for ear training*, away from performance.
- **Tromlitz, *Ausführlicher und gründlicher Unterricht die Flöte zu spielen* (Leipzig, 1791); Powell's translation *The Virtuoso Flute-Player* (Cambridge UP, 1991).** Covers intonation, articulation, posture, ornaments. Rachel Brown (2002) characterises it: "He provides note-by-note guidance on intonation in every scale." Bowers (1994), *Performance Practice Review* 7(1), "Tromlitz on Playing the Flute: a Résumé," summarises his intonation teaching; I could not retrieve the PDF (not verified), so quote Tromlitz only after reading Powell.
- **Hotteterre, *Principes de la flûte traversière* (Paris, 1707); Lasocki's translation *Principles of the Flute, Recorder and Oboe* (1968).** The first flute method; concise; fingering charts, tu/ru articulation, ornaments. On intonation it instructs correcting individual notes by embouchure: "tune by the embouchure. To lower it, roll the flute inward" (Lasocki pp. 16–17, as quoted by Breithaupt, *Flute Talk*, Oct. 2014). It does not describe a learning method beyond this and ordered fingering instruction (Brown 2002 calls it "far more open-minded" than Quantz, encouraging the reader to choose fingerings that work).
- **Haynes (1991), "Beyond temperament: non-keyboard intonation in the 17th and 18th centuries," *Early Music* 19, 357–381.** Winds and singers used a flexible, roughly 1/6-comma-meantone intonation with flats a comma above sharps, learned by ear and by reference to keyboards; woodwind fingering charts from c. 1700 distinguish enharmonic pairs.
- **Modern traverso pedagogy.** Rachel Brown, *The Early Flute: A Practical Guide* (Cambridge UP, 2002; ch. 3 "Technique"); Brown notes that Boland's *Method for the One-Keyed Flute* (Univ. of California Press, 1998) is the only modern systematic tutor. Barthold Kuijken, interview in *Flute Talk* (April 2015): work early on "getting the forked fingerings like F natural or A flat in the first octave to sound well and in tune"; "the forked fingerings will not work when you blow too strongly"; the instrument "tells you how to play, or maybe rather how not to play it." Kuijken, *The Notation Is Not the Music* (Indiana UP, 2013) and Powell, *The Flute* (Yale UP, 2002) are standard references but I did not verify intonation-specific content in them this session. I found no empirical study of traverso intonation learning.

**Findings to act on:** the treatises teach intonation as ear + embouchure correction of a known map of defective notes; they expect the ear to be trained against a reference (bass line, keyboard, monochord) outside of performance; and Quantz's practice regime is bounded, rested, slow, and imitative.

## Design implications for this app

**Already right, per the evidence**
1. No needle during the note, reading after: consistent with Swinnen et al. 1990, Schmidt & Wulf 1997, and Pardue & McPherson's distraction findings.
2. Drone/bass as the reference: matches Quantz X §15 ("especially the bass") and Haynes; but say plainly in the document that controlled short-term tests (Springer 2021, Zabanal 2019) found no immediate accuracy benefit and inflated confidence, and that there is no long-term trial.
3. Comparing the player to their own best: aligns with Kluger & DeNisi (avoid self-focused/normative feedback), Henderlong & Lepper (competence "without overreliance on social comparisons"), and SDT's informational-not-controlling criterion.
4. No points/badges/streaks: grounded by Deci et al. 1999 (contingent rewards), Hanus & Fox 2015, Mekler et al. 2017, and the instability of motivational effects in Sailer & Homner 2020.
5. A known "note bend asymmetry" (your memory notes: low C won't go up, F sits sharp) is exactly Quantz IV §23's map of naturally high/low notes; presenting corrections per note in Quantz's terms (turn in/out, less/more air) is historically and pedagogically apt.

**Could add**
1. **Estimate before reveal.** After a note ends, ask "sharp, flat, or in tune?" (optionally "by how much") *before* showing the reading. This is the error-detection mechanism of Swinnen et al. and Hewitt's self-evaluation, and it converts every-trial KR into something closer to KR-as-check. (Guadagnoli & Kohl 2001, *J. Motor Behavior*, on error estimation and KR frequency, surfaced in search but was not read; not verified.)
2. **Reduce feedback frequency as the player improves.** Offer summary feedback after a phrase or a block of notes, and bandwidth feedback (silence when within tolerance, a reading only outside it), with the bandwidth widening as consistency improves (Sherwood 1988; Schmidt et al. 1989; Winstein & Schmidt 1990). Frame the change as "your ear is taking over," which is true.
3. **Feedback after good trials.** Where the reading is positive, say so first (Chiviacowsky & Wulf 2007), while noting internally that this effect is now considered overstated (McKay et al. 2023); it costs nothing and fits the ethic.
4. **A short audiation step.** "Hear it, sing it (or hum it), then play it" before a target, and a warm-up that combines imagery, singing and playing (Steenstrup et al. 2021; Quantz X §20 on playing back what you hear). Keep claims modest: evidence supports perception and combined practice, not singing alone.
5. **Interleave exercises and vary keys/registers** within a session, and tell the player it will feel worse and be better (Stambaugh 2011; Carter & Grahn 2016; Abushanab & Bishara 2013). The daily warm-up already in CR-003 is the natural place.
6. **Space across days, not minutes.** Return to the same targets on later days (Rubin-Rabson 1940; Simmons & Duke 2006); do not claim within-session spacing benefits (Wiseheart et al. 2017).
7. **Bounded sessions with rests.** A gentle "that is a good place to stop" after roughly an hour of focused work reflects Ericsson et al. 1993 and Quantz's rests; no streaks, no penalties for stopping.
8. **A monochord-style "ear calibration" mode**, separate from exercises: play the reference, let the player hear and label semitone sizes and the flat-above-sharp distinction (Quantz on the monochord via Haynes 1991), with the detector used as a teacher's reference, not a performance meter.
9. **Thinking time.** Do not rush the next prompt; a deliberate pause before each attempt is a marker of expert practice (Palese & Duke 2022).
10. **Temperament claims stay modest.** Even experts default to one intonation regardless of context (Kopiez 2003); report drift toward a target rather than insisting on just vs tempered thirds.

## Do not claim

- "The 10,000-hour rule." Gladwell's number; Ericsson et al. 1993 never proposed a threshold and Ericsson later disowned it (Ericsson & Pool, *Peak*, 2016; not re-verified). Deliberate practice explains about a fifth of variance in music (Macnamara et al. 2014).
- "Growth-mindset messaging improves results." Weak, heterogeneous, and bias-affected effects (Sisk 2018; Burnette 2023; Macnamara & Burgoyne 2023). Use process-focused wording because it is decent, not because it works.
- "Learning styles" (visual/auditory learners). No supporting evidence (Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer & Bjork 2008, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*; well known, not re-verified this session).
- "Drones improve intonation" as an established finding. Players believe it; three short experiments found no accuracy effect.
- "Tuners ruin the ear." Not demonstrated; the one removal test in music (Blanco et al. 2021) found visual-feedback gains retained. The defensible statement is that continuous displays divert attention and, in general motor learning, degrade retention.
- "Singing before playing fixes intonation." Null for performed intonation in several studies; positive for perception and combined practice.
- "Immediate feedback is always best." The opposite for retention on simple tasks (Swinnen 1990).
- "Letting learners choose when to see feedback boosts learning." Large recent replications are null (St. Germain 2022; McKay 2023).
- "Gamification is proven harmful." Average effects are small-positive; the honest claim is unstable motivational benefit, documented harms, and a mechanism that threatens intrinsic motivation.
- "Flow causes achievement." Correlational (O'Neill 1999).
- "More practice hours = better." Duke, Simmons & Cash 2009: quality of error handling, not time.
- "Quantz said X" about anything not in the transcription quoted above; in particular I could not verify Tromlitz's exact wording or the Schlegel & Springer results, and neither should be cited until read.

**Items to check before publication:** Schlegel & Springer 2018 results; Sigrist et al. 2013 body text; Bowers 1994 on Tromlitz; Mathias & Goldman results; Guadagnoli & Kohl 2001; the Pashler 2008 and Ericsson & Pool 2016 citations.
